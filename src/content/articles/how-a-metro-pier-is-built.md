Building an elevated metro line through a crowded city is one of the most demanding civil engineering operations. You have live traffic on either side, underground utility lines crisscrossing below the asphalt, and tight barricades measuring only a few meters wide. Every elevated track segment is carried by heavy reinforced concrete columns known as metro piers.

The construction starts deep underground with large-diameter bored piles. Hydraulic rotary drilling rigs bore through soil and rock while bentonite slurry keeps the open borehole walls from collapsing. Once the drill reaches hard bedrock, workers lower a prefabricated cylindrical rebar cage into the hole and pump high-strength self-compacting concrete using a tremie pipe.

After four or six piles are completed, workers excavate the top dirt and cast a thick pile cap block. This cap ties all the separate pile tops together into one rigid structural unit. Heavy starter rebar bars extend straight upward out of the cap, providing the lap connection for the circular or rectangular column that will rise into the sky.

Next, ironworkers assemble the pier rebar cage. Metro piers carry immense vertical weights and must resist severe earthquake shaking, so they feature dense longitudinal bars bound by tightly spaced spiral hoops. Once the cage passes safety inspections, crane operators lower modular steel shuttering panels around the rebar, bolting the forms securely into position.

Concrete is pumped into the shuttering in continuous lifts with internal immersion vibrators eliminating air pockets. After forty-eight hours of initial curing, the steel forms are unbolted and crane-lifted away, revealing a smooth concrete column. A wide pier cap is cast at the very top, ready to support the precast viaduct girders and passing trains.
